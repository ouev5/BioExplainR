Detailed Methods
ASM Cell Culture and Treatment
Primary ASM cells were isolated from white non-smoking donors, 9 who died of fatal asthma 
and 8 with no chronic illness or medication use. ASM cell cultivation was as described 
previously (1, 2). Briefly, passages 3 to 4 ASM cells maintained in Ham's F12 medium 
supplemented with 10% FBS, CaCl2, buffered with HEPES, penicillin/streptomycin, primoch, 
and additional L-glutamine were used in all experiments. The F12 medium was used for culture 
because it provides Ca2+ levels that are consistent with seeing contractility of muscles in that 
media. Cells from each donor were treated with 100 nM budesonide or control vehicle (DMSO) 
for 24 hours.
RNA-Seq Library Construction and Sequencing 
Total RNA was extracted from cells using the miRNAeasy mini kit (Qiagen Sciences, Inc., 
Germantown, MD). Using approximately 1 µg of RNA from each sample, the Illumina TruSeq 
Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) was used to prepare 
poly(A)-selected stranded RNA-Seq libraries, where second-strand cDNA synthesized with 
dUTP was digested, and only first-strand cDNA existed in the library. Ambion External RNA 
Controls Consortium (ERCC) RNA Spike-In Control Mix 1 (Life Technologies Corporation, 
Carlsbad, CA) was added to the samples. Amplified cDNA fragments were analyzed using the 
2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA) to determine fragment quality 
and size. Library concentrations were determined by Qubit Fluorometric Quantitation (Life 
Technologies Corporation, Carlsbad, CA). An Illumina HiSeq 2500 instrument was used to

generate 125 bp paired-end reads at the University of Pennsylvania Next-Generation Sequencing 
Core (Philadelphia, PA). 
RNA-Seq Data Analysis
Taffeta scripts were used to analyze RNA-Seq data and generate HTML summary reports 
(https://github.com/blancahimes/taffeta) (3, 4). Adapter sequences in raw reads were trimmed 
out using Trimmomatic (v.0.32) (5), and overall QC metrics were obtained using FastQC (6) 
(v.0.11.7). Trimmed reads for each sample were aligned to the reference hg38 genome and 
known ERCC transcripts using STAR (7) (v.2.5.2b), while constraining mapped reads to be 
within reference hg38 or ERCC transcripts. Bamtools (v.2.3.0) (8) was used to summarize the 
number of mapped reads, including junction spanning reads. The Picard Tools (v.1.96; 
http://picard.sourceforge.net) RnaSeqMetrics function was used to compute the number of bases 
assigned to various classes of RNA, according to an hg38 refFlat file obtained via a UCSC 
Genome Table. HTSeq (v.0.6.1, with the strand setting ‘-s reverse’) was used to quantify both 
ERCC Spike-In and gene-level read counts, using provided hg38 human transcriptome and 
ERCC reference files (9). ERCC Spike-in dose-response curves (i.e., plots of ERCC transcript 
read counts vs. known quantity of ERCC transcript molecules added to samples) were created 
following the manufacturer’s protocol (10). Gene-level differential expression analysis that 
compared (1) budesonide vs. control in non-asthma donor-derived ASM, (2) budesonide vs. 
control in fatal asthma donor-derived ASM, (3) fatal asthma vs. non-asthma donor-derived ASM 
exposed to control, and (4) fatal asthma vs. non-asthma donor-derived ASM exposed to 
budesonide, was performed under a negative binomial distribution model with DESeq2 
(v.1.18.1) (11) while filtering out genes with a total read count <10 before differential analysis

and disabling the default option for independent filtering of differential expression results. Cell 
donor was used as a covariate in the differential expression analysis of glucocorticoid vs. control 
comparison; sex and age were used as covariates in fatal asthma vs. non-asthma donor-derived 
ASM comparisons. Ensembl gene IDs were annotated to official gene symbols using BioMart 
(2016 archive) (12). Genomic coordinates were retrieved from Ensembl Release 84 (March 
2016). RNA-Seq data is available in GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession 
GSE94335.
Gene Set Enrichment Analysis
Gene set enrichment analysis was performed with the fast gene set enrichment analysis algorithm 
implemented in the fgsea R package (v.1.4.1) (13). Gene sets of KEGG and Reactome pathway 
annotations were downloaded from MsigDB collections (v.6.2) 
(http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/collections.jsp) and those with gene number >15 
and <500 were included for analysis. Genes were sorted according to decreasing z-score from 
DESeq2 results. If multiple Ensembl gene IDs were annotated to the same gene symbol, that 
with the largest z-score was retained. Gene-level score for a gene in rank k was calculated by 
adding a positive or negative value, based on the presence or absence of the gene in the gene set, 
to the score of the gene in rank k-1, and positive values were weighted by the gene k’s z-score. 
Enrichment score for a particular gene set was designated as the largest absolute value from all 
gene scores. P-values were assessed based on the distribution of enrichment scores following 
10,000 permutations, and subsequently, q-values were obtained using the Benjamini-Hochberg 
approach. The leading-edge subsets, defined as genes in the ranked list that appear before and at 
the position of a positive gene-set enrichment score or that appear at and after the position of a

PAGE 5
negative gene set enrichment score, correspond to genes that drove the enrichment signal. 
Dependent pathways with q-value <0.05 were collapsed using the collapsedPathways function in 
fgsea.
Analysis of Publicly Available Glucocorticoid-Response Transcriptomic Datasets 
We sought publicly available transcriptomic datasets that measured the effect of glucocorticoid 
exposure on various human cell types to determine ASM-specific transcriptomic changes 
induced by glucocorticoids. GEO searches of the terms “glucocorticoids”, “budesonide”, 
“dexamethasone”, and “fluticasone” in Homo sapiens, yielded 13 glucocorticoid response 
datasets (12 microarray and one RNA-Seq). Differential expression results of glucocorticoid vs. 
control comparison for individual studies were obtained using RAVED 
(https://github.com/HimesGroup/raved). For the RNA-Seq study, raw sequence data in fastq 
format was downloaded from SRA and taffeta scripts were used as described for the primary 
ASM studies above. For microarray studies, GSEMatrix and raw data files were downloaded 
from GEO. A quality control step obtained quality metrics of raw intensity data using outlier 
scoring methods from the arrayQualityMetrics R package (14). Scan date was considered as a 
batch variable via principal component analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed 
with the limma R package (15) using robust multi-array average (RMA)-processed intensity data 
(16). For studies with noticeable batch effects, the sva R package (17) was used to obtain 
adjusted statistics. The Benjamini-Hochberg approach was used to correct for multiple 
comparisons made. Microarray probes were annotated to official gene symbols using R array 
annotation packages corresponding to the appropriate platform. For cases in which multiple 
probe or Ensembl gene IDs annotated to the same official gene symbol, the smallest p-value was

retained for that gene symbol. Full differential expression results for each of these individual 
studies is available in our previously developed app REALGAR (http://realgar.org/) (18).
Tissue-Specific Integration of Glucocorticoid-Response Transcriptomic Data
For cell types that had more than one transcriptomic dataset, we obtained integrated results using 
two summary statistics-based approaches: (1) an effect size-based method that applied a random-
effects model with heterogeneity of selected datasets estimated by DerSimonian and Laird’s 
method (19), and (2) a rank-based method that adopted the Rank Product method (20). For (1), 
effect sizes measured as log2 fold change and standard errors from individual studies were passed 
to the meta.summaries function from the rmeta R package (21) to compute combined effect sizes 
and standard errors. Corresponding p-values were derived from resultant z-score distributions 
and converted to q-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg approach. For (2), genes present in 
each individual dataset were ranked according to their unadjusted p-values. Rank product 
statistics were computed using ranks re-scaled by the total number of genes. The approximate 
rank product distribution was obtained using 106 permutations, and empirical p-values were 
defined based on the proportion of simulated rank products that were smaller than the observed 
rank product. Corresponding q-values were conservatively estimated using false discovery rates. 
Gene significance per cell type was determined based on having a q-value <0.05 in both effect-
size- and rank-based integration procedures. Full R code used for integration analysis is available 
at https://github.com/HimesGroup/Asthma_Transcriptomic_Analysis.

Hierarchical clustering was performed to compare cell-type specific gene expression patterns 
across 15 glucocorticoid vs. control comparisons available among the 14 transcriptomic studies 
considered (including the comparisons for fatal asthma and non-asthma donor-derived ASM 
described above). The genes selected to perform clustering were 56 significantly differentially 
expressed genes in response to budesonide exposure in non-asthma ASM that also had results 
available in all datasets considered. Study and gene expression similarity were determined based 
on the Euclidean distance between log2 fold changes in expression as implemented in the 
heatmap.2 function in the gplots R package.
Detailed RNA-Seq Quality Control Results
Thirty-four RNA samples from 17 ASM donors, 9 with fatal asthma and 8 non-asthma controls, 
with and without budesonide exposure, were obtained (Table E1). For these 34 samples, we 
obtained an average of 111.3 million trimmed sequencing reads per sample (range 87.9-312.0 
million reads per sample). An average of 91.8% of RNA-Seq reads aligned to hg38 genome 
reference files downloaded from Illumina’s iGenomes project (range 84.2%-94.7%), and an 
average of 34.6% of the mapped reads spanned junctions (range 31.9%-36.9%). An average of 
94.1% of bases in mapped reads corresponded to mRNA (range 86.5%-95.3%). Plots of 
normalized read coverage of transcripts vs. normalized position revealed that there was even 
coverage of transcripts (Fig. E1A). ERCC spike-in dose response plots, which ideally would 
have slope and R2 equal to 1.0, had an average slope and R2 of 0.95 (range 0.92-0.99) and 0.94 
(range 0.90-0.95) respectively (Fig. E1B shows the plot for a representative sample). Two 
housekeeping genes (GABARAP and RPL19) were used as positive controls for sequencing, and 
as expected, they were highly expressed viz-a-viz their normalized read counts and levels did not
