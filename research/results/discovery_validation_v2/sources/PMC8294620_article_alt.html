Abstract

Supplemental O2 (hyperoxia) is necessary for preterm infant survival but is associated with development of bronchial airway hyperreactivity and childhood asthma. Understanding early mechanisms that link hyperoxia to altered airway structure and function are key to developing advanced therapies. We previously showed that even moderate hyperoxia (50% O2) enhances intracellular calcium ([Ca2+]i) and proliferation of human fetal airway smooth muscle (fASM), thereby facilitating bronchoconstriction and remodeling. Here, we introduce cellular clock biology as a novel mechanism linking early oxygen exposure to airway biology. Peripheral, intracellular clocks are a network of transcription-translation feedback loops that produce circadian oscillations with downstream targets highly relevant to airway function and asthma. Premature infants suffer circadian disruption whereas entrainment strategies improve outcomes, highlighting the need to understand relationships between clocks and developing airways. We hypothesized that hyperoxia impacts clock function in fASM and that the clock can be leveraged to attenuate deleterious effects of O2 on the developing airway. We report that human fASM express core clock machinery (PER1, PER2, CRY1, ARNTL/BMAL1, CLOCK) that is responsive to dexamethasone (Dex) and altered by O2. Disruption of the clock via siRNA-mediated PER1 or ARNTL knockdown alters store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) and [Ca2+]i response to histamine in hyperoxia. Effects of O2 on [Ca2+]i are rescued by driving expression of clock proteins, via effects on the Ca2+ channels IP3R and Orai1. These data reveal a functional fASM clock that modulates [Ca2+]i regulation, particularly in hyperoxia. Harnessing clock biology may be a novel therapeutic consideration for neonatal airway diseases following prematurity.

Keywords: airway hyper-responsiveness, airway smooth muscle, asthma, clock biology, prematurity

INTRODUCTION

Provision of supplemental O2 even at moderate levels (<60% O2) to premature infants (<34 weeks gestation) in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) is a necessary intervention to maintain oxygenation but the relative hyperoxic exposure early in life (1–7) is associated with subsequent development of bronchial airway hyperreactivity (AHR) and asthma (2, 8–11). Preterm infants who received supplemental O2 show AHR within the first year of life (12) and resultant diminished lung function and airway thickening are risk factors for childhood asthma (13, 14). Additionally, airways in preterm infants are narrower and more susceptible to obstruction, e.g., from structural changes (15–17). Thus, understanding mechanisms by which developing airways are impacted by early insults such as hyperoxia may provide novel future interventions to prevent life-long lung diseases.

Airway smooth muscle (ASM) is a vital cell type for airway tone as well as structure across the lifespan and is involved in the pathophysiology of diseases such as asthma. Previous studies have demonstrated that insults such as hyperoxia affect developing ASM in the context of airway structure and function. Using human fetal ASM (fASM) from 18–22-week (canalicular stage) lungs, we previously showed that moderate hyperoxia (50% O2) increases ASM intracellular calcium ([Ca2+]i) and proliferation (18), and extracellular matrix deposition (19). We have also shown that fASM express a range of [Ca2+]i regulatory proteins, including pathways such as intracellular sarcoendoplasmic (SR) Ca2+ release/reuptake channels and plasma membrane store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) involving STIM1 and Orai1, as well as inositol trisphosphate receptor (IP3R) and transient receptor potential channel (TRPC3), allowing for fASM to respond to agonists (e.g., histamine and acetylcholine) comparable to adult ASM (18). Additionally, neonatal mice exposed to moderate O2 exhibit increased ASM thickness, collagen deposition, and response to methacholine challenge (20). However, mechanisms underlying detrimental neonatal O2 effects on the airway are still being explored.

We propose cellular clock biology links early oxygen exposure to development of asthma. Clocks are accredited for maintaining circadian rhythmicity in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), but peripheral clocks and their disruption are now considered key players in numerous diseases, including asthma. Peripheral clocks, expressed in several cell types including in the lung, are intracellular networks of transcription-translation feedback loops that produce circadian oscillations of clock genes and their targets (clock-controlled genes) (21, 22). Although peripheral clocks have been implicated in the pathophysiology of adult asthma (23, 24), clock biology in the developing airway is largely unknown. However, premature infants suffer circadian disruption (22, 25) whereas conversely light-entrainment strategies improve outcomes (e.g., increased weight gain and decreased time-to-discharge) (8). The core clock machinery (see discussion and schematic) consists of ARNTL/BMAL1 (aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator-like protein 1; a key element for oscillations), CLOCK (circadian locomotor output cycles kaput), CRY1/2 (cryptochromes 1 and 2), and PER1/2 (Period 1 and 2). BMAL1 and CLOCK regulate CRY1/2 and PER1/2 gene expression whereas PERs and CRYs accumulate in the cytoplasm before translocating back to the nucleus where they inhibit BMAL1:CLOCK transcriptional activity, thereby inhibiting their own expression in an approximate 24 h cycle. The clock can be manipulated by a multitude of signals that happen to be important in airway biology and asthma including nuclear receptors and glucocorticoids (26, 27), metabolic byproducts (28), and cAMP/Ca2+ signaling (29). Conversely, the clock itself regulates many downstream targets (30) involved in calcium regulation (31, 32), proliferation (25, 33, 34), inflammation (35–38), energy metabolism (23, 39, 40), and mitochondrial dynamics (41): processes highly relevant to asthma pathophysiology and ASM functionality. The bidirectional relationship between the clock and its output pathways is highlighted by the relationship between the clock and cAMP/Ca2+ signaling in neuronal cells: increased cAMP from activated adenylate cyclase and increased [Ca2+]i from plasma influx and SR release subsequently increases PER1/2 gene expression via cAMP response elements (CREs) in promoter regions of neuronal cells (29) whereas SCN neurons exhibit daily oscillations in [Ca2+]i, L-type Ca2+ currents, and firing frequencies (42). In recent years, another source of cross-talk with the clock has been identified: O2. Clocks are evolutionarily linked to O2-sensing such that metabolic needs match O2 availability. Recent studies show rhythmic O2 oscillations (16%/21% in vivo; 5%/8% in vitro) synchronize cellular clocks (43) and circadian disruption deleteriously impacts O2 sensing and metabolic regulation (44). These studies emphasize a novel connection between clocks and O2, which may be relevant in the context of oxygen insults during the perinatal period. There is limited information on clocks in developing lung (45), although clocks in adult airway diseases have received recent attention (46–48). Lung clocks are likely important given need for optimal lung function at birth but there is lack of information regarding perinatal airway clock patterns and rhythmicity, or relevance to cell functionality and pathways relevant to disease pathogenesis.

We hypothesized that fASM have an intact and responsive clock network that has a function important for ASM: [Ca2+]i regulation. We proposed that hyperoxia alters clock function and conversely the clock can be leveraged to attenuate deleterious effects of O2 on [Ca2+]i. Using human fASM, we investigated clock machinery in the context of O2, effect of clock disruption via siRNA mediated knockdowns on [Ca2+]i, and whether leveraging clock pathways via overexpression constructs was sufficient to attenuate effects of O2 on [Ca2+]i.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human ASM

Human fASM were enzymatically dissociated from tracheobronchial trees of 18–22-week gestation fetuses following demise, as previously described (18, 49). fASM acquisition are considered exempt by Mayo Institutional Review Board (IRB), with samples entirely de-identified, and no involvement of investigators in acquisition of the tissues. Cells are characteristically smooth muscle, as they express key markers and regulatory elements involved in [Ca2+]i regulation (18, 19, 50). Both sexes were used in experiments involving fASM (12 females and 7 males) ranging in age from 67 days to 127 days. Adult human ASM cells were obtained from patients undergoing elective thoracic surgery at Mayo Clinic, as previously described (18), and are approved under Mayo IRB #08–002518. Adult ASM used were classified as normal/benign airway and from non-asthmatics. Both sexes were used (3 females, 3 males) and ranged in age from 33 to 77 years.

Animal Studies

Male and female C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME) were used in protocols approved by Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. After breeding adult mice, pregnant dams were observed for signs of pregnancy and birth. On day of birth, neonates were put in either 21% or 50% O2 for the first 7 days of life followed by 2 weeks of recovery in 21% O2. Nursing mothers were rotated daily between O2 conditions to avoid maternal oxygen toxicity and water/food was ad libitum. Twenty-one-day-old neonates were euthanized and lungs inflated with paraformaldehyde and paraffin embedded for sectioning (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded, FFPE).

Immunostaining

Neonatal lung sections (8 males and 11 females) obtained from autopsy paraffin-embedded airways of subjects who died within two weeks of birth (Mayo IRB#15–007416) (51) and adult lung sections obtained from patients undergoing elective thoracic surgery (Mayo IRB#08–002518) were de-paraffinized and immunostained for BMAL1 (1:200; Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, MA; 14020; RRID AB_2728705), α-smooth muscle actin (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, MA; ab21027; RRID AB_1951138; αSMA), or DAPI. A Nikon Eclipse Ti2 fluorescence microscope was used to obtain images at 400× after image acquisition parameters were set using background controls at each wavelength.

Cell Culture

fASM were cultured before passage 7 in DMEM/F12 (Life Technologies, Rockville, MD) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic. For synchronization, fASM were pulsed with 100 nM dexamethasone (Dex) or Vehicle (0.01% EtOH) for 30 min in growth medium containing 10% FBS, washed with sterile PBS, and replenished with growth medium to allow for cycling. For the subset of fASM exposed to hyperoxia, 40% O2 exposure began at the time of Dex pulse and continued until data collection at designated time points. fASM transfected with siRNA or vehicle (see below) were exposed for 24 h in either 21% or 40% O2 immediately after transfection and serum deprived 24 h later (total hyperoxia exposure = 48 h). For fASM exposed to hypoxia-mimic CoCl2, growth medium was supplemented with 100 µM CoCl2 for 24 h and cells were serum deprived with 0.5% FBS medium containing 100 µM CoCl2 for an additional 24 h.

siRNAs

siRNAs were obtained from Dharmacon (Lafayette, CO): Human ON-TARGETplus SMARTPool ARNTL (L-010261-00-0020), PER1 (L-011350-00-0020), Non-targeting Control Pool (D-001810-10-50), siGLO Green Transfection Indicator (D-001630-01-20). fASM were transfected using RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) per manufacturer’s protocols.

Plasmids

Human tagged ORF clone plasmids were obtained from OriGene (Rockville, MD): BMAL1-pCMV6-GFP (NM_001178) transcript variant 1 (RG207870); PER1-pCMV6-GFP (NM_002616) homolog 1 (RG224777); pCMV6-AC-GFP Mammalian Expression Vector with C-terminal GFP tag (PS100010). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 per manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen).

Gene Expression

RNA was isolated (RNeasy Mini Kit; Qiagen, Germantown, MD; 74104), cDNA synthesized (Transcriptor Reverse Transcriptase; Roche, Indianapolis, IN; 03531287001), and Quantitative Real Time PCR performed (Roche Light Cycler 96 System) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche; 4707516001). Primers sequences listed below, and data were analyzed using the comparative C(t) [ΔΔC(t)] method and S16 as the housekeeping gene.

Protein Expression

fASM exposed to hypoxia-mimic CoCl2, 21% O2, or 60% O2 for 48 h were harvested for protein using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher #89901) and PMSF protease inhibitor (Cell Signaling Technologies, #8553) following manufacturer’s instructions. Protein quantification was done using BCA assay (Thermo Fisher #23225) and expression analyzed by automated digital Western blot technology, Wes (Protein Simple, San Jose, CA) for BMAL1 (Abcam, ab93806, 1:100) and GAPDH (Cell Signaling, #2118, 1:400). Quantitative analysis was performed using Simple Western software.

Live-Cell [Ca2+]i Imaging

fASM were loaded with 5 µM Fura-2/AM (ThermoFisher) for 30 min at room temperature in Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; 2 mM Ca2+, pH 7.4). Cells were imaged on an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti-U) using standard Fura-2 filters. Cells for quantification were chosen using manually drawn ROIs marking fASM positive for siGLO or GFP. 10 µM histamine or 10 μM acetylcholine was used as agonist and perfused during imaging and baseline, peak, and amplitude of [Ca2+]i responses were assessed.

SOCE experiments were performed as previously described (52) by perfusing cells sequentially with 2 mM Ca2+ HBSS, 0 Ca2+ HBSS with 1 µM nifedipine and 10 mM KCl to stabilize membrane potential, addition of 10 µM CPA under these conditions to deplete SR Ca2+ stores, and finally rapid re-introduction of 2 mM Ca2+ HBSS in the continued presence of CPA to initiate SOCE.

Statistical Analyses

All experiments used at least four fASM patient samples (n = one patient line). Data were analyzed using either ratio paired t-test, one- or two-way ANOVA, or mixed effects analysis with Tukey’s multiple comparisons, where appropriate, in GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Outliers were determined by Grubbs’s outlier test. Data are represented as mean ± SEM and P < 0.05 used for statistical significance.

RESULTS

Expression of Clock Components in Developing Airway

Intracellular clock networks and their role in airway disease are well established in adult lung cell types (46–48), but their expression and classical clock functions are not understood in the developing airway. Using fASM, core clock gene expression (PER1, PER2, CRY1, CLOCK, and ARNTL) was analyzed and compared to ASM isolated from adult airways. Expression of these core clock genes was found to be comparable between fASM and adult ASM, except for notably higher CRY1 in fASM (Fig. 1A). Lung sections from premature infants or healthy adults were immunostained for BMAL1, α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA), and DAPI. BMAL1 was found to be expressed in the smooth muscle layer as well as the epithelium. Although the adult airway epithelium expressed BMAL1 to a larger extent than in smooth muscle, neonatal ASM showed relatively higher BMAL1 expression in ASM compared to epithelium (Fig. 1B) justifying a focus on fASM in this study.

BMAL1 is evolutionarily linked to O2-sensing pathways via HIF-1α in terms of structural similarities in their PAS domains and the clock has been shown to exhibit a reciprocal relationship with HIF-1α in terms of transcription factor functionality (44, 53). To determine whether various levels of O2 would alter BMAL1 expression, fASM were exposed to 48 h of 21% O2, hypoxia mimic CoCl2, or 60% O2 before protein isolation and Wes analysis. BMAL1 expression increased in fASM exposed to 60% O2 compared to lower O2 conditions (Fig. 1C). To evaluate whether effects of O2 can be recapitulated in vivo, WT neonatal mice were subjected to either 21% or 50% O2 from the day of birth to 7 days post birth and followed by 14-day recovery at 21% O2. This setup matches the timing of early insults in premature babies with day 21 in mice representing when children present with wheezing at <3–4 years of age. FFPE sections of day 21 neonatal mouse lungs were immunostained for BMAL1 or αSMA, which showed increased BMAL1, most notably in the airway epithelium, of neonatal mice subjected to hyperoxia for the first 7 days of life (Fig. 1D). These data highlight an impact of O2 on BMAL1 and potentially the clock overall in both human fASM and a neonatal hyperoxia mouse model.

fASM clock responsiveness to Dex pulse in the context of hyperoxia

In adult cells, a functional clock is classically demonstrated by the responsiveness of its machinery to synchronizing agents, such as Dex (54, 55). Without external cues (e.g., Dex), in vitro culture of mammalian cells will autonomously oscillate, but not in synchrony (56). To determine whether the fASM clock networks are responsive to Dex, fASM were pulsed with 100 nM Dex or vehicle control (0.01% EtOH) for 30 min and harvested 12, 16, 20, and 24 h later (e.g., t24 = 24 h post-Dex pulse) for gene expression analysis. In fASM receiving vehicle control pulse at 21% O2, fASM did not show changes in PER1 gene expression from t12 to t24 whereas PER2 increased at t16 to t24 compared to t12, albeit not significantly (Fig. 2A). ARNTL decreased in expression from t16 to t24 compared to t12 whereas CLOCK and CRY1 displayed increased expression at t24 compared to t12 (Fig. 2A). Dex pulse resulted in decreased PER1 gene expression at t12 compared to Veh control and increased PER1 gene expression at t20 whereas ARNTL showed an opposite effect (increased expression at t12 and decreased at t24) (Fig. 2A). Similarly to Veh control, Dex pulse fASM showed increased CLOCK and CRY1 expression at t24 (Fig. 2A). In a subset of experiments, fASM were incubated in 40% O2 starting at the time of Dex or Veh pulse and for the remainder of the time course. In hyperoxia, PER1 gene expression was decreased in the Veh control group compared to normoxic Veh control and PER1 expression peaked in response to Dex at t16 in hyperoxia, earlier than in normoxic Dex pulse (Fig. 2B). Notably, PER2 gene expression was increased in hyperoxia in both Dex and Veh pulse groups compared to normoxia, suggesting a unique effect of O2 on PER2 (Fig. 2B). Additionally, O2 increased ARNTL expression in Veh control group and its expression decreased in both Dex and Veh groups in hyperoxia, like the expression pattern in normoxia. Last, CLOCK and CRY1 expression peaked at t16 in the hyperoxic Veh pulse group, earlier than in normoxia, and expression dropped back to baseline by t24. This effect was similarly seen in the hyperoxic Dex pulse group, which was not observed with Dex pulse in normoxia (Fig. 2B). Because clock responsiveness has been predominately examined in adult cells, we used adult ASM from male and female non-asthmatic donors to measure clock gene responsiveness to Dex or Veh pulse as a comparison to fASM. Adult ASM showed increased PER1 and PER2 gene expression at t24 in response to Dex compared to Veh control pulse whereas ARNTL expression increased at t12 and decreased by t24 in the Dex pulse group compared to Veh control group (Fig. 2C). CLOCK and CRY1 did not display noticeable changes in gene expression in response to Dex pulse during the time points examined in adult ASM (Fig. 2C).

Disrupting the fASM Clock Alters [Ca2+]i Regulation in the Context of O2

To identify a functional role of the clock in fASM, siRNA-mediated knockdowns of PER1, ARNTL, or non-targeting control (Scrambled, Scr) were generated. Sufficient knockdown was validated by qPCR, showing ∼80–90% PER1 knockdown compared to Scr siRNA and ∼50% ARNTL knockdown compared to Scr siRNA over a 24 h period (Fig. 3A). To ensure siRNA transfection did not alter clock responsiveness, transfected fASM were pulsed with Dex and gene expression analyzed. fASM transfected with Scr siRNA exhibited increased PER1 gene expression at t40 and t48 and increased ARNTL gene expression at t32 with a subsequent dip in expression by t40 (Fig. 3B). The clock profile of clock genes PER2, CRY1, and CLOCK in the knockdown lines was evaluated and found neither PER2, CRY1, or CLOCK were upregulated because of PER1 or ARNTL knockdown (Fig. 3C).

We previously showed that fASM [Ca2+]i response to agonists is elevated in hyperoxia in a dose-dependent manner (18). Additionally, interactions between the clock and O2-sensing pathways have been noted in the context of basic cellular functions and homeostasis (43, 44, 57–59). Although Ca2+ signaling has been shown to regulate the clock (29) and the clock itself regulates downstream targets involved in calcium regulation (31, 32), the link between clock networks, O2, and [Ca2+]i in fASM is unknown. To determine whether disruption of the clock via PER1 or ARNTL knockdown would affect [Ca2+]i regulation in an O2-dependent manner, fASM were co-transfected with fluorescent transfection indicator siGLO (Fig. 4A) to identify positively transfected cells during live cell microscopy using Fura-2/AM. PER1 knockdown, ARNTL knockdown, or Scr siRNA fASM incubated in either 21% or 40% O2 for 48 h were imaged during perfusion with HBSS and histamine. Responses were measured for calculation of baseline, peak, and amplitude. Scr siRNA transfection did not alter [Ca2+]i response to histamine or acetylcholine compared with untransfected fASM (Fig. 4B). fASM [Ca2+]i response to acetylcholine increased in 40% O2 compared to 21% O2, which was not altered in either PER1 or ARNTL knockdown fASM (Fig. 4C). In 21% O2, neither PER1 nor ARNTL knockdown fASM showed altered [Ca2+]i response to histamine compared to Scr. However in 40% O2, ARNTL knockdown fASM had significantly increased [Ca2+]i response to histamine compared to Scr siRNA fASM in the same O2 exposure (Fig. 4D). Surprisingly, PER1 knockdown fASM revealed significantly decreased [Ca2+]i response to histamine in 40% O2 compared with Scr siRNA fASM (Fig. 4D).

In additional experiments, PER1 knockdown, ARNTL knockdown, or Scr fASM co-transfected with siGLO were used during the SOCE protocol. In 21% O2, PER1 knockdown significantly increased SOCE following SR depletion with trending increased SOCE in ARNTL knockdown fASM compared to Scr siRNA fASM (Fig. 4E). Additionally, ARNTL knockdown increased the rate (slope) of SOCE influx compared to Scr siRNA fASM, with a trend toward significance in PER1 knockdown fASM, all performed in 21% O2 (Fig. 4E). Representative tracings are shown. In 40% O2, loss of ARNTL showed a trending decrease in SOCE and loss of PER1 did not alter influx of SOCE. Additionally, slope of SOCE influx was unaltered in the knockdown fASM compared to Scr (Fig. 4E). These data show that loss of ARNTL increases [Ca2+]i to histamine only in hyperoxia whereas loss of PER1 decreases [Ca2+]i response to histamine only in hyperoxia, and loss of either ARNTL or PER1 increases SOCE following SR Ca2+ depletion in normoxia whereas loss of ARNTL decreases SOCE influx in hyperoxia.

Leveraging the Clock to Rescue Effects of O2 on Intracellular Calcium Regulation

We next sought to determine whether BMAL1 or PER1 could rescue the deleterious effects of O2 on [Ca2+]i response to histamine. fASM were transfected with GFP-tagged BMAL1 plasmid cDNA construct (BMAL1-GFP), GFP-tagged PER1 plasmid cDNA construct (PER1-GFP), or GFP-tagged empty vector control (pCMV6-GFP). GFP-positive fASM were selected for measuring [Ca2+]i response to histamine (Fig. 5A). Forty percent O2 increased [Ca2+]i response to histamine in the pCMV6-GFP fASM, as expected. In 21% O2, BMAL1 overexpression did not alter [Ca2+]i response to histamine whereas PER1 overexpression showed a trending decrease in amplitude response (Fig. 5B). In 40% O2, whereas BMAL1 overexpression did not alter hyperoxia effects on [Ca2+]i response to histamine, PER1 overexpression significantly decreased [Ca2+]i to histamine compared to pCMV6-GFP control fASM in hyperoxia (Fig. 5B). Representative tracings are shown. Transfection with Lipofectamine 2000 did not significantly alter calcium response to histamine (Fig. 5C). To assess whether BMAL1 or PER1 function through IP3R or TRPC3, transfected fASM were treated with either IP3R inhibitor XeC (Fig. 5D), TRPC3 inhibitor Pyr3 (Fig. 5E), or vehicle control (DMSO) and calcium responses to histamine measured. Data show effects of PER1 may function in part through IP3R.

Because loss of ARNTL and PER1 increased rate of SOCE and SOCE influx in normoxia, respectively, and loss of ARNTL or PER1 did not alter effects of hyperoxia on SOCE influx (Fig. 4E), we next determined whether overexpression of BMAL1-GFP or PER1-GFP would attenuate the deleterious effect of hyperoxia on this mechanism. fASM positively transfected with BMAL1-GFP, PER1-GFP, or pCMV6-GFP were selected for live-cell imaging during the SOCE perfusion protocol. BMAL1-GFP or PER1-GFP (Fig. 6A) overexpression did not alter SOCE or rate (slope) of SOCE in 21% O2. However, in 40% O2, PER1-GFP significantly decreased slope of SOCE compared to pCMV6-GFP with BMAL1-GFP slope trending toward significance in hyperoxia (Fig. 6A). Representative tracings are shown.

To understand mechanisms of clock-controlled [Ca2+]i regulation in hyperoxia, BMAL1-GFP, PER1-GFP, or pCMV6-GFP transfected fASM exposed to 40% O2 were treated with Orai1 inhibitor, AnCoA4, or vehicle control (DMSO) for 1 h before the SOCE protocol. As expected, AnCoA4 decreased SOCE overall. However, while PER1-GFP decreased SOCE influx and rate of SOCE in hyperoxia, this effect was abolished in the presence of AnCoA4 (and effect not seen with BMAL1-GFP) (Fig. 6B). These data suggest PER1-mediated reduction in SOCE involves Orai1.

DISCUSSION

Understanding mechanisms of elevated risk of developing chronic airway disease for premature infants given supplemental O2 is vital to improving long-term outcomes. The evolutionary connection between the clock and O2 sensing pathways, as well as evidence showing the relevance and importance of circadian entrainment in the NICU led us to propose a functional role for the clock in the developing airway. Here, we show that fASM express core clock machinery, comparable to adult ASM, and are responsive to Dex. Our data with PER1 or ARNTL knockdown to model clock disruption and determine necessity of these clock components in fASM functionality show differential effects where loss of ARNTL increased [Ca2+]i in hyperoxia whereas loss of PER1 decreased [Ca2+]i, and in normoxia loss of either gene increased SOCE. Interestingly, there is no straightforward reciprocal relationship between increased versus decreased expression of clock genes in fASM, perhaps reflecting the immaturity of the system. However, O2 increased PER2 expression suggesting there may be a compensatory mechanism in hyperoxia independent of siRNA knockdowns (e.g., increased PER2 expression may explain decreased [Ca2+]i to histamine in PER1 knockdown fASM in hyperoxia). Overexpression of BMAL1-GFP did not alter [Ca2+]i response to histamine regardless of O2 exposure, but did decrease SOCE in 40% O2. Interestingly, PER1-GFP overexpression decreased [Ca2+]i response to histamine and SOCE, and its effects appear to involve IP3R and Orai1, identifying these channels as mechanistic links to clock regulation of [Ca2+]i in fASM (Fig. 7).

Since the discovery and subsequent exploration of peripheral clocks emerged, the primary focus has been on clock networks in the adult lung and adult diseases of the airway. Adult lung function exhibits circadian patterning in healthy individuals in terms of forced expiratory volume, peak flow, and airway resistance (60). Therefore, there are notable circadian patterns in asthmatic symptoms and responsiveness to treatment (61–63). Additionally, environmental factors such as altered light-dark cycles, cigarette smoke, and viral or bacterial infections have all been implicated for their detriment to lung clocks and downstream pathways regulating lung function (35, 36, 38, 64–67). In contrast to these clinical and research data in adults, very limited information is available regarding peripheral clocks in fetal and neonatal development, and far less is known with respect to fetal lung clocks. The relevance of clock networks during fetal development was once questioned because of lack of embryonic lethality or any striking differences in newborn pups from animal models deficient in clock genes, however there are notable effects of clock deletion animal models in adulthood: for example, whole-body BMAL1 deficient mice lack circadian rhythmicity (68), are infertile (both males and females, 69, 70), and show signs of early aging and mortality (68, 71, 72). In a conditional BMAL1 mouse model where BMAL1 was present during embryogenesis and fetal development but deleted postnatally, many characteristics of early aging and mortality in the classic BMAL1 knockout mouse are abolished (72). This highlights a critical role for clock networks during fetal development that sets the stage for health or disease in adulthood, but our mechanistic understanding of this concept is quite frankly in its infancy.

Maintaining appropriate oxygen tension in the fetal in utero environment is critical for normal fetal lung development. The in utero environment is relatively hypoxic with fetal pO2 around 20–30 mm Hg (73, 74). Not only are O2 demands lower for the fetus, but less O2 is necessary for normal lung branching morphogenesis and angiogenesis. Therefore, preterm birth alone subjects the lung to higher O2 than needed and interrupts normal progression of lung development, and supplemental O2 administered to these premature infants only exacerbates this effect (75). A key factor to consider in the in utero environment is hypoxia inducible factor (HIF)-1 α. HIF-1α is critical for fetal development (knockout animals are embryonic lethal) (76) and HIF-1α has a bi-directional relationship with the clock through heterodimerization with BMAL1 to regulate downstream target genes (77) and through BMAL1 regulation of HIF gene expression (53). HIFs can also be upregulated by hyperoxia (78), emphasizing the robust nature of cellular O2-sensing pathways. Additionally, recent studies have highlighted the power of the O2-clock relationship by showing rhythmic O2 exposure in vitro “resets” the clock in a HIF-1α-dependent manner (43). Taking these findings into consideration, our data showing fASM responsiveness to Dex and unique effects of 40% O2 on clock gene expression compared to 21% O2 highlight the complex relationship between O2-sensing pathways and clock networks. Furthermore, there may be other factors at play in neonatal lung injury such as oxidative stress and inflammation because clock networks have been emphasized for their role in these pathways. For example, another core circadian component, nuclear receptor REV-ERBα, has been shown to help cellular responses to hyperoxia-driven oxidative stress via NrF2 and inflammation via NFκB, which was done using a mouse model of neonatal hyperoxia-driven lung injury (79). This mechanism may be unique to the neonatal lung because NFκB can quickly activated in the hyperoxic neonatal lung compared to the adult lung (80, 81), providing explanation for higher O2 tolerance in neonatal animal models than hyperoxic injury animal models in adults (82). Additionally, it is unlikely that fASM function independent of surrounding cell types, such as the airway epithelium, which can modulate ASM proliferation and remodeling through secretory mechanisms (i.e., smooth muscle mitogens). Therefore, effects of hyperoxia on airway epithelium clock networks and potential subsequent downstream effects on developing ASM need to be considered in future studies. The beneficial or detrimental role of clock networks in the neonatal lung and clock involvement in O2-sensing pathways, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response in the context of O2 need further investigation.

PER1 and PER2 clock genes contain CREs in their promoter regions and can therefore be regulated by cAMP and Ca2+ signaling, which has been shown in neuronal cell types (29). Manipulation of [Ca2+]i pathways via chronic elevation or inhibition of Ca2+ channels alters neuronal clock oscillation (i.e., amplitude, phase, and period of the clock) (29). In yet another bidirectional relationship, clock networks also regulate the function of multiple channels such as cGMP-gated channels, L-type and T-type voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, and K+ channels (83). Circadian regulation of ion channels has been explored primarily in neuronal cell types, and our data suggest this relationship exists in non-neuronal cell types, as well. Increases in cAMP resulting from Ca2+ influx through L-type voltage-gated channels regulates the clock directly at CREs in promoters of clock genes and the clock itself regulates adenylyl cyclase activity (83, 84). Additionally, increased generation of secondary messenger cAMP activates downstream pathways via protein kinase A (PKA), which regulate ion channel function downstream, either through Ras-Erk-CaMKII signaling or through PI3K-Akt signaling, both of which regulate ion channel functions (83). Our data indicate the fASM clock regulates calcium channel function uniquely in hyperoxia and through Orai1 to regulate SR Ca2+ stores. Based on data from other cell types, it is plausible the fASM clock network additionally involves other plasma membrane and/or SR Ca2+ channels (influx via TRPC3; SR Ca2+ release via IP3/PLC/IP3R and CD38/cADPR/RyR pathways) to regulate [Ca2+]i and contractility.

Interestingly, our data showed decreased [Ca2+]i response to histamine in hyperoxia with both PER1 knockdown as well as PER1-GFP overexpression. This effect was not seen with ARNTL knockdown and BMAL1-GFP experiments. Although one possible explanation for the PER1 results is that its homolog PER2 is a retained compensatory mechanism (BMAL1 is, however, the only non-redundant clock component that lacks a compensatory homolog), our data did not support this claim as PER2 gene expression was not upregulated in the knockdown fASM. However, O2 exposure alone upregulated PER2 gene expression across all time points measured, suggesting that the apparent protective effect of PER1 knockdown may be through PER2 compensatory mechanisms in hyperoxia. Because PER1 knockdown increased SOCE in normoxia (not hyperoxia), these data emphasize the complexity of clock controlled [Ca2+]i regulation that is O2-dependent. Furthermore, hyperoxia alone appeared to induce potential gene expression oscillations of CLOCK, and CRY1 as evidenced by emergence of peak gene expression at t16 in compared to t24 in normoxia. These findings highlight that there is clearly more exploration necessary to understand chronic versus pulsed O2 to understand links between clocks, O2, and fASM cellular behvarior. Future studies will explore the relationship between the clock and O2-sensing pathways (whether O2 can be used to leverage clock networks in fASM) and the function of clocks in additional pathways involved in neonatal airway disease (contractility, proliferation, mitochondrial function, and metabolism).
